## Part 1 — Who is it for?

**Workflow:**

> People who are **reviewing stock positions they own — checking whether their original reason for buying still holds, and deciding whether to keep, revise, or exit**

This is the neglected audit loop. Almost every investor should do it; almost nobody does it well. The setup and evidence-capture workflows feed into it, but the review moment is where the pain concentrates.

**Competitive alternative:**

> relying on **memory and scattered notes they never re-read**

The real competition is not Notion or a spreadsheet. It's the absence of a system. Most investors trust their memory for why they own something. The more disciplined ones have an Apple Notes doc or a Notion page — but they never revisit it.

**Problem:**

> and that sucks because **six months later they can't remember why they bought the stock, they hold losers too long because the original reason is buried, and they keep making the same mistakes without realizing it**

## Part 3 — Differentiation

> **And here's what we do differently:**

1. **You write down what must be true before you start collecting articles.** The app makes you state your assumptions upfront — what has to hold for the thesis to work, and what would prove you wrong. No note app requires this.

2. **Every article or data point gets linked to a specific assumption** — not just filed by date or by stock. You can instantly see which assumptions have evidence and which are blind spots.

3. **The app flags when your evidence is one-sided.** If 90% of what you've saved is bullish, it tells you. Confirmation bias becomes visible instead of invisible.

4. **Everything is timestamped.** Six months from now, you see what you actually believed — not what you think you believed. You can't rewrite history.

5. **You get prompted to revisit positions on a schedule.** Keep it, revise it, or kill it. Nothing goes stale.

6. **Everything stays on your Mac.** No cloud, no accounts, no tracking. Your reasoning never leaves your machine.

## Objection Handling

**"Isn't this just Notion with a template?"**
You could build a stock research database in Notion. But Notion won't require you to state your assumptions before collecting evidence. It won't flag that 90% of your evidence is bullish. It won't remind you to review stale positions. And it won't show you — six months later — what you actually believed versus what you think you believed. Notion stores pages. Hyppo tracks the status of an argument.

**"I already use a trading journal (TradeZella, Journalytic)."**
Trading journals track trades — entries, exits, P&L, emotional state at the time of execution. Hyppo tracks reasoning — the assumptions behind your decisions, the evidence for and against, and how your conviction evolved *before* you ever place a trade. It's the "why" layer that trade journals skip entirely. They're complementary, not competing.

**"Why can't I just do this in a spreadsheet?"**
A spreadsheet can store tickers and buy prices. It can't enforce structure on your thinking, link evidence to specific assumptions, detect one-sided research, remind you to review stale positions, or give you an honest record of what you believed six months ago. The spreadsheet is a filing tool. Hyppo is a thinking tool.

**"I don't need structure — my process works fine."**
If you can look at every stock you own right now and immediately explain (a) the specific assumptions your thesis depends on, (b) what evidence would prove you wrong, and (c) whether your evidence is balanced or one-sided — then your process is working and you don't need Hyppo. Most people who say this can do it for their favorite position but not the other nine.

## Quick Validation Tests

1. **10-second comprehension** — Show the assembled statement to 5 investors cold. Ask them to repeat back what it does. Target response: "It's a journal that makes you write down why you own a stock and then checks if you're still right." If they say something about "tracking conviction" or "managing research" — too abstract; positioning didn't land.

2. **Replacement test** — Ask: "If I deleted your Apple Notes / Notion stock notes and gave you Hyppo, would your research output be the same or better?" Target answer: "Better, because I'd actually revisit things and I'd have to write down my assumptions." If they say "about the same" — the value prop isn't distinct enough.

3. **Willingness-to-pay proxy** — Ask: "Would you pay $5/month for an app that prevents you from holding a stock for six months past the point your original thesis broke?" If the visceral answer is "yes" or "I'd pay more" — the pain is real and monetizable. If they hesitate — the problem isn't felt sharply enough.
