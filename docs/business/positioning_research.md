# Positioning Research — Multi-Level Drill-Down

This document breaks each positioning question into multiple levels of specificity. The goal is to map the full option space so the positioning statement picks the sharpest, most concrete answers — not the broadest.

---

## 1. Workflow — "People who are ..."

### Level 0 — The entire job (too broad for positioning)

- "Researching stocks and managing investment theses."

This is the whole app. Equivalent to Loom saying "for people who communicate at work." Useless for earning a slot in someone's head.

### Level 1 — The four research loops

Each loop is a distinct recurring activity. They happen at different frequencies and carry different levels of pain.

| Loop | Activity | Frequency | Pain level |
|------|----------|-----------|------------|
| Setup | Writing down a thesis for a stock — what you believe and why | Per new position (weekly–monthly) | Medium — most people skip this entirely |
| Capture | Saving a piece of evidence (article, data point, quote) you just read | Daily (for active researchers) | High — friction kills retention |
| Update | Revisiting a thesis after new information arrives | Event-driven (earnings, news) | High — most people never do this |
| Audit | Reviewing a position to decide keep, revise, or exit | Scheduled (weekly–quarterly) | Very high — "zombie positions" fester |

### Level 2 — Calendar-level activities (narrowest, most concrete)

These are things that would show up on a calendar or in a daily routine. Each one could be a positioning entry point.

**Within Setup:**
- Writing down why you want to buy (or already own) a stock.
- Listing the assumptions that must be true for the thesis to work.
- Defining what specific data would prove you wrong.

**Within Capture:**
- Saving a link or quote from an article you just read.
- Noting a key number from an earnings call transcript.
- Clipping a piece of evidence and marking whether it supports or contradicts your thesis.

**Within Update:**
- Checking whether new earnings data changes your thesis.
- Deciding if a news event breaks your original investment logic.
- Updating your confidence after reading contradicting evidence.

**Within Audit:**
- Reviewing a position you haven't looked at in weeks or months.
- Deciding whether to hold, add to, reduce, or exit a position.
- Looking back at what you believed six months ago versus what actually happened.
- Preparing a research summary to share or export.

### Positioning pick — which workflow?

For a prosumer/PLG product, pick Level 2. The three strongest candidates:

1. **"Reviewing a stock position"** — highest pain, strongest structural differentiation. Happens on a cadence. Everyone who owns stocks should do this; almost nobody does it well.
2. **"Saving notes about a stock you're researching"** — highest frequency. The daily use case. But this is the workflow where general note apps compete most directly.
3. **"Writing down why you're buying a stock"** — strongest "hypothesis-first" angle. But it's a one-time event per position, which makes it less recurring.

**Recommendation:** Lead with the review moment ("Reviewing a stock position") because it's the most painful, most neglected, and most differentiated. The setup and capture workflows support it.

## 2. Competitive Alternative — "using ..."

### Level 0 — The behavior being replaced (not a tool name)

- **Trusting memory and gut feeling** (doing nothing structured).
- **Taking scattered notes** (low-effort documentation).
- **Using a formal system** (only professionals at firms).

For most of our target audience, the real alternative is not a specific tool. It is doing nothing systematic.

### Level 1 — Specific tools/behaviors per segment

**Retail investor who doesn't document:**
- Trusting memory. "I know why I own it."
- Checking a portfolio app (Robinhood, Schwab) for P&L and calling that "research."
- Reddit / Twitter / YouTube for ideas — consumption with no documentation.

**Retail investor who takes some notes:**
- Apple Notes or Google Docs — one note per stock, unstructured text.
- Notion — database of stocks, maybe with tags or a template.
- Spreadsheet — tickers, buy prices, target prices, maybe a "notes" column.
- Physical notebook.

**Semi-pro / independent analyst:**
- Word or Google Docs — investment memos.
- Email to self / Slack DMs as a research archive.
- Shared drive with PDFs, screenshots, and notes.

**Professional at a fund:**
- Enterprise RMS (Bipsync, Verity) for compliance and storage.
- Internal wiki or Notion workspace.
- Bloomberg/FactSet notes (data-attached, not thesis-attached).

### Level 2 — What they literally do (most concrete)

- "Copy-paste an article link into Apple Notes with a one-line thought."
- "Keep a spreadsheet row per stock with buy price and a reason column."
- "Write a Notion page with a bulleted list of pros and cons."
- "Screenshot a chart and paste it into a Slack DM with themselves."
- "Do nothing. Just remember."
- "Write a multi-page Google Doc investment memo that nobody (including them) ever re-reads."

### Positioning pick — which alternative?

The talk says: name the behavior, not a tool list. Loom said "meetings," not "Zoom, Google Meet, or Teams."

The honest alternative for most target users is: **trusting their memory and maybe keeping a few scattered notes.** The more specific version for the note-taking minority is: **a personal Notion page or Apple Notes doc they never re-read.**

**Recommendation:** Lead with the behavior: "relying on memory and scattered notes." This encompasses both the "I don't write anything down" crowd and the "my notes are a graveyard" crowd. It's honest and immediately recognizable.

## 3. Problem — "and that sucks because ..."

### Level 0 — The meta-problem

Investors make the same mistakes repeatedly because they have no system for learning from their own decisions.

### Level 1 — Structural problems (what's actually broken)

1. **Reasoning is scattered** — thesis, evidence, and decisions don't live in the same place.
2. **No revision history** — can't see how thinking evolved over time.
3. **No review trigger** — positions go stale without anyone noticing.
4. **Evidence is unbalanced** — confirmation bias is invisible because nobody tallies sentiment.
5. **Setup is skipped** — people collect evidence without first defining what they're looking for, so evidence is noise.

### Level 2 — Felt, visceral pains (what customers would actually say)

- "I forgot why I bought this stock."
- "I held way too long — the original reason was gone but I didn't notice."
- "I kept reading bullish articles and ignored the warning signs."
- "I look back and think I predicted it, but my actual notes tell a different story."
- "My notes are a mess — I can't find what I wrote about [company] three months ago."
- "I don't have notes. I just buy and hope."
- "Every stock I own, I could explain the bull case but couldn't name a single bear argument."
- "I never revisit old positions. They just sit there."
- "I spent months researching and still couldn't explain my thesis in two sentences."

### Problem type (from the talk's framework)

- **Workflow problem** (the activity is broken): Most investors don't have a research discipline at all. They need to start doing something they currently don't do. This requires selling the workflow before the tool.
- **Tool problem** (the tool is broken): For the disciplined minority who already take notes, their tools aren't designed for thesis tracking. The tool fails them. This is an easier sell: "keep doing what you're doing; do it here instead."

For most of our audience it's a **workflow problem**. Implication: the positioning needs to sell the habit ("you should be writing down your assumptions") before it sells the tool ("and here's where to do it"). This is harder but also less competitive — there's no incumbent to displace if nobody is doing it.

### Positioning pick — which problem?

Lead with the most visceral Level 2 pain: **"Six months later you can't remember why you bought the stock, you hold losers too long because the original reason is buried, and you keep making the same mistakes without realizing it."**

This is felt, specific, and doesn't require the customer to know what "hindsight bias" or "thesis drift" mean.

## 4. Category — "We are a ..."

### Level 0 — Broad (too generic)

- Productivity app
- Research tool
- Note-taking app

Useless. No slot earned.

### Level 1 — Scoped to audience (findable, understood)

| Category | Pros | Cons |
|----------|------|------|
| Investment journal | Most searchable term; immediately understood | Commoditized — many simple journaling apps exist |
| Stock research app | Clear; implies utility | Could be confused with screeners or data terminals |
| Research notebook for investors | More specific; "notebook" implies persistence | "Notebook" might undersell the structure |
| Investment research tracker | Descriptive | Clunky; nobody would search for this |

### Level 2 — Differentiated (adds mechanism to category)

- "An investment journal that tracks your reasoning, not just your trades."
- "A stock research app that forces you to write down what would prove you wrong."
- "A research notebook that tells you which of your assumptions have no evidence."

### Category strategy (from the talk)

**Mature category (e.g., "investment journal"):** Easier comprehension, less education needed. But harder to differentiate against existing journal apps. The Loom path: pick the boring, instantly understood category and differentiate with the "what we do differently" bullets.

**Emerging category (e.g., "conviction tracker"):** More novelty/buzz, but heavy education burden. No pricing anchor. Risk it doesn't stick.

**Recommendation:** Follow the Loom playbook. Pick the plain category — **"investment journal"** — and let the differentiation bullets do the work. Nobody needs to be taught what a journal is. "Investment" scopes it. Done.

## 5. Differentiation — "And here's what we do differently ..."

### By degree (same thing, dramatically better)

These position Hyppo as "a better note app for investors." Lower differentiation, easier comprehension.

- Faster evidence capture than Notion (global hotkey, auto-detect clipboard content).
- Better organization than a spreadsheet (hierarchical: company → thesis → assumption → evidence).
- More searchable than Apple Notes (full-text search, filters by date/tag/entity type).

### Binary (fundamentally different — competitors don't do this at all)

These position Hyppo as a new kind of tool. Higher differentiation, requires more explanation.

- **You write your assumptions before collecting evidence.** No note app requires this. Hyppo makes you say "what must be true?" before you start clipping articles.
- **Every piece of evidence is linked to a specific assumption.** Not filed by date or by stock — by the exact claim it supports or contradicts. Notion can't do this.
- **The app flags blind spots automatically.** Assumptions with zero evidence get surfaced. One-sided evidence (90% bullish) gets flagged. No note app or spreadsheet does this.
- **Your reasoning is timestamped and immutable.** You can't retroactively rewrite what you believed. Six months later, you confront your actual past logic, not your memory of it.
- **Scheduled reviews prompt keep / revise / kill.** The app nudges you to revisit stale positions. No note app will do this.
- **100% local, no cloud.** Privacy is architecture, not just a policy toggle. Nothing leaves your Mac.

### Positioning pick — which flavor?

Lead with binary differentiators. The "by degree" points (faster, better organized, more searchable) could describe any specialized note app. The binary points describe something no existing tool does.

Rewrite binary points in customer language (no product jargon):

1. **Write down what must be true before you start collecting articles.** The app won't let you skip this step.
2. **Every article or data point gets linked to a specific assumption** — so you can instantly see which ones are supported and which are blind spots.
3. **The app flags when your evidence is one-sided.** If 90% of what you've saved is bullish, it tells you.
4. **Everything is timestamped.** Six months from now, you see what you actually believed — not what you think you believed.
5. **You get prompted to revisit positions on a schedule.** Keep it, revise it, or kill it — nothing goes stale.
6. **Everything stays on your Mac.** No cloud, no accounts, no tracking.
